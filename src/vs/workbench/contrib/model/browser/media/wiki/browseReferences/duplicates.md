# Browse References — Duplicates and Merging

## How Browse References Finds Possible Duplicates
**Possible Duplicates** lists pairs of works that may be one: the same first author, years within two of each other, and similar titles or the same title, one with a subtitle. Such pairs arise when a work is saved under a slightly different title, or from a preprint and its published version. The list is a suggestion, as two papers by one author can have similar titles and both be real.

## What Happens to the Keys When Works Are Merged
**Merge...** makes a pair into one work. You pick the record whose details to keep with **Keep This One**, and then **Confirm**. The merged work keeps the key that sorts first, and the other key becomes another key for it, so that panels citing it still find it; the work shows **Also cited as** with that key. **Undo Merge** separates them again.

## The Theory Behind Possible Duplicates in a Reference Library
Duplicates among references are a case of record linkage. [Fellegi and Sunter (1969)](#ref-FS1969a) sort each pair of records into link, non-link and possible link, the last left to a person; **Possible Duplicates** in Browse References is that third class. [Elmagarmid, Ipeirotis and Verykios (2007)](#ref-EIV2007a) explain why the task is hard: duplicates share no common key, and differ through transcription errors, incomplete information and the lack of standard formats. Removing them pays off in screening, as [Bramer, Giustini, De Jonge, Holland and Bekhuis (2016)](#ref-BGDHB2016a) note for systematic reviews, where each duplicate left in adds to the reviewers' workload.
