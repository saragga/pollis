# Snowball Search Map — Links and Citations

## How Snowball Search Map Ranks the Pool
The pool is ranked by **Links**, the number of seeds a work is tied to, and then by citations. A work tied to several seeds is likely to sit close to all of them, whereas a raw citation count favours the famous work on any subject. In the Links column, sorting takes the links first and then the filled dots.

## Links, Bibliographic Coupling and Co-Citation
Counting ties to the seeds rests on two older measures of closeness between works. A work that several seeds cite is a reference they share, the situation [Kessler (1963)](#ref-Kes1963a) named bibliographic coupling between those seeds. A work that cites several seeds cites them together, the situation [Small (1973)](#ref-Sma1973a) named co-citation. In both, the shared tie suggests that the works belong together, and a high Links value marks a work at the centre of such ties.

## Reading the Dots of the Links Column
Each seed tied to a work appears as a dot, the hover names the ties, and clicking the dots opens the details under the work. A filled dot is a citation Semantic Scholar finds influential, and a hollow dot any other citation. **≈** marks a seed the work is only related to, which Links does not count. The filled dots rest on the idea that some citations build on a work while others only mention it, as in [Valenzuela, Ha and Etzioni (2015)](#ref-VHE2015a).

## Asking Semantic Scholar About the Citations
**Ask Semantic Scholar About the Citations** fetches, for the works in the pool, the sentences that cite a seed, why they cite it (Background, Method or Result) and whether the citation is influential. Reading those sentences is a quick way of telling a work that uses a seed's method from one that merely mentions it.

## Why a Citation Between Two Works Is Not Agreement
Works cite for different reasons. [Cohan, Ammar, van Zuylen and Cady (2019)](#ref-CAZC2019a) classify citation intent, in their dataset SciCite, into background information, use of methods and comparing results, so that a link between two works says little until the passage is read. Chains of citations can also mislead: in a network of 242 papers and 675 citations, [Greenberg (2009)](#ref-Gre2009a) traced citation bias, amplification and invention, which together gave a claim unfounded authority. A heavily linked work at the top of Snowball Search Map is thus a place to start reading, and not evidence that its claims hold.
