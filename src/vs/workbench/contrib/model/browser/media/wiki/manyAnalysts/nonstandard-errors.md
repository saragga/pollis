# Many Analysts: Nonstandard Errors

What the spread of the estimates across analysts measures, how it compares with the standard error each analyst reports, and what the published many-analysts studies found.

## What Is a Nonstandard Error?

A standard error measures the uncertainty of an estimate that comes from drawing a sample from a population. [Menkveld and 342 coauthors (2024)](#ref-MDHHJ...2024a) name a second source of uncertainty: the variation in how researchers turn the same data into evidence, which they call the evidence-generating process. The nonstandard error is the uncertainty that this variation adds, measured across researchers who test the same hypothesis on the same data. Many Analysts reports it as the standard deviation of the estimates across the analysts, under the name **Nonstandard Error**.

## How Does the Nonstandard Error Compare with the Standard Error?

Many Analysts sets the **Nonstandard Error** beside the **Median Standard Error**, the middle of the standard errors the analysts reported, and says how many times the one is the other. A ratio near zero means the analysts' choices hardly moved the estimate, and the standard error describes the uncertainty well. A ratio near one or above means that the choice of analysis moves the estimate as much as sampling does, which the standard error of a single study does not show. [Huntington-Klein and 11 coauthors (2021)](#ref-HABBB...2021a) found the standard deviation of estimates across replications to be 3 to 4 times the mean reported standard error.

## What Did the Published Many-Analysts Studies Find?

| Study | Field | Analysts | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Silberzahn and 64 coauthors (2018)](#ref-SUMAA...2018a) | Psychology | 29 teams, 61 analysts | Odds ratios from 0.89 to 2.93; 20 teams found a significant effect and 9 did not |
| [Huntington-Klein and 11 coauthors (2021)](#ref-HABBB...2021a) | Economics | 7 replicators for each of 2 studies | No two reported the same sample size; for one study the effect's sign varied |
| [Breznau and 165 coauthors (2022)](#ref-BRWNA...2022a) | Sociology | 73 teams, 161 researchers | More than 95% of the variance of the results stayed unexplained by the teams' coded decisions |
| [Menkveld and 342 coauthors (2024)](#ref-MDHHJ...2024a) | Finance | 164 teams | Sizable nonstandard errors, smaller for more reproducible or higher rated research, and reduced by peer review |

## What Explains the Spread Across Analysts?

The published studies looked for the source of the spread in the analysts themselves and mostly did not find it. In [Silberzahn and 64 coauthors (2018)](#ref-SUMAA...2018a), neither the analysts' prior beliefs nor their expertise readily explained the variation, and neither did peer ratings of the analyses' quality. In [Breznau and 165 coauthors (2022)](#ref-BRWNA...2022a), expertise, prior beliefs and expectations barely predicted the outcomes. [Menkveld and 342 coauthors (2024)](#ref-MDHHJ...2024a) found that participants underestimated this kind of uncertainty. What remains is the many defensible choices of an analysis, each reasonable, whose combinations lead to different numbers.

## How to Read the Spread of Many Analysts

**The Spread** gives the **Median Estimate**, the **Interquartile Range**, the **Range**, the **Nonstandard Error** and the **Median Standard Error**. The median and the interquartile range resist a single analyst far from the rest; the range and the nonstandard error do not, so an outlier shows as a wide range beside a narrow interquartile range. **The Estimates** plots each analyst's estimate and 95% confidence interval in order, over a band for the interquartile range and a dashed line at the median: intervals that do not overlap mean analysts who would draw different conclusions. With 3 to 12 analysts every figure of the spread is itself uncertain, so it describes this study's analysts rather than all possible analyses.

## Which Choices Move the Estimate in Many Analysts?

**The Choices** lists, for each analyst, the estimate, its standard error, its interval and its number of observations, beside the five kinds of choice each analyst records in `choices.toml`: **Sample**, **Outcome**, **Controls**, **Estimator** and **Standard Errors**. Set beside the plot, which has the analysts in order of their estimates, the table shows which choices go with the high estimates and which with the low ones: in a job training study, for example, the comparison group taken as the sample. With a few analysts this is a pattern to look into, not a measured effect of the choice.
