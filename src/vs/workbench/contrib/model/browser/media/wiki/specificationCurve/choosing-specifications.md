# Specification Curve: Choosing the Specifications

How the decisions and options of a specification curve are gathered, which to keep, and how many specifications they make.

## What Are Decisions and Options in a Specification Curve?

A decision is a point of an analysis where a researcher must choose, such as which comparison group to use, which observations to drop, which controls to include or which estimator to apply. An option is one answer to a decision. A specification is one option of each decision, so the number of specifications is the product of the numbers of options: three decisions with three, four and two options make 24 specifications. Specification Curve keeps each decision and option with an id of lower-case letters, digits and underscores, which `specification.jl` receives, and a label, which the page shows. A decision with only one option kept fixes that choice for every specification.

## Which Options Belong in a Specification Curve?

[Simonsohn, Simmons and Nelson (2020)](#ref-SSN2020a) ask for the specifications that are theoretically justified, statistically valid and non-redundant. An option belongs when a careful researcher could defend it before others in the field, not when it is merely possible. Two options that give the same rows and the same estimate add nothing but weight to one part of the curve, so one of them should be unticked. [Steegen, Tuerlinckx, Gelman and Vanpaemel (2016)](#ref-STGV2016a) gather the choices of excluding, transforming and coding the data, which are often the least visible ones. Under **The Choices**, hovering an option shows the reasons its proposers gave, and unticking it leaves it out of every specification.

## Why Does the Outcome of a Specification Curve Keep One Unit?

Every specification must estimate the same quantity in the same unit, the one **Estimate to Report** names, or the curve would sort numbers that cannot be compared. A different measure of the outcome, such as its logarithm in place of its level, changes what the estimate means, and is better studied as a curve of its own. For the same reason a Many Analysts study gives no outcome decision. The standard error is not a decision either: each specification reports its own, which the curve uses for its confidence intervals and the test for its statistics.

## How Does Specification Curve Merge the Agents' Proposals?

Each proposer writes its `decisions.toml` on its own, and the page merges them by id: a decision or an option of the same id from two proposers is shown once, with both proposers' numbers in **Proposed By** and both reasons in its tooltip. Proposers often give one idea under different ids, as two options or two decisions. **Same As...** in the **Change** column merges an option into another of its decision, or a decision into another, whose options then join it; the one merged into keeps its id and label, and gains the proposers and reasons of the other. **Rename** changes a label, **Drop** leaves a decision out of the code and the count, and **Restore** brings it back; **Undo Last Change** takes back the latest change. The changes are kept in `edits.json` of the study, and the code is written from the choices they give. An option proposed by every agent is usually central; one proposed by a single agent may be a useful idea the others missed, or one to leave out. The skill asks each proposer for the options a careful researcher would defend, and for the reason of each.

## How Does Specification Curve Take the Choices of a Many Analysts Study?

**A Many Analysts Study** under **Choices From** takes the choices of each analyst of a finished Many Analysts study, from its `choices.toml`, in place of asking agents. The analysts' samples, controls and estimators become the options of three decisions, and analysts who wrote the same words share one option. The question, the estimate and the data are the study's, so that the options fit them. The curve then shows every combination of the analysts' choices, including the combinations no analyst used, which separates the effect of each choice from the analyst who made it. [Menkveld and 342 coauthors (2024)](#ref-MDHHJ...2024a) call the spread of estimates across research teams the nonstandard error; a curve of the analysts' choices helps see which choices it comes from.

## How Many Specifications Can a Specification Curve Have?

The count under the table of decisions changes as options are ticked and unticked. **Write the Code** accepts at most 2,000 specifications, so that the curve and the test finish in minutes. Each decision multiplies the count, so a curve grows quickly: one more option of a decision with three adds a third to the count. Every specification runs once for **The Curve**, and once for each resample for **Inference Under the Null**, so 2,000 specifications with 500 resamples run a million estimates. Merging redundant options, or dropping a decision that matters little, keeps the curve to its informative part.
