# Referee Report: From Reports to a Decision

How the editor of Referee Report weighs the referees' reports, why referees disagree, what the decision letter holds, and how to read the Decision step.

## Why Do the Referees of the Same Paper Disagree?

Disagreement among referees is the rule in peer review rather than the exception. [Cicchetti (1991)](#ref-Cic1991a), across disciplines, finds consistently low agreement between referees, and reports that referees of grant proposals agree much more about what does not deserve support than about what does. [Bornmann, Mutz and Daniel (2010)](#ref-BMD2010a) pool 70 reliability coefficients from 48 studies of journal peer review, covering 19,443 manuscripts, and find a mean Cohen's kappa of 0.17 and a mean intra-class correlation of 0.34 between independent reviews of the same manuscript. [Berk, Harvey and Hirshleifer (2017)](#ref-BHH2017a) see in the disagreement among referees of economics journals a sign of arbitrariness in the review process. The referees of Referee Report work alone for the same reason a journal's do: their disagreements show the editor where the paper's case is open.

## How Does the Editor of Referee Report Weigh the Reports?

The editor of Referee Report weighs the referees' arguments, not their votes. It lists the major concerns of every report, merges those that are the same and names every referee who raised each, and decides from the concerns that would change the paper's claims and from whether the authors can resolve them. When it decides against most referees, the letter says so and why. Where referees disagree, the letter says on what, and which view the editor takes. A concern of the editor's own, in no report, is marked Editor. The editor keeps every section, table, page and quotation as the reports and the draft give it, and checks it against the draft when it matters to the decision.

## What Does the Decision Letter of Referee Report Hold?

| Section of `LETTER.md` | What it holds |
|---|---|
| **Decision** | Accept, Minor Revision, Major Revision or Reject, in the title |
| **The Paper** | The paper's question, what it does and what it finds, as the editor reads it |
| **The Decision** | Why, from the concerns that decide it |
| **Where the Referees Disagree** | On what, and the view the editor takes; left out when they agree |
| **Required Revisions** | What the authors must do, each with the referees who raised it and what would satisfy it; for Reject, what would make the paper publishable, if anything would |
| **Suggested Revisions** | What would improve the paper without deciding it |
| **The Referees' Recommendations** | Each referee's role, recommendation and confidence |

The referees' minor points stay in their reports, which go with the letter.

## How Do I Read the Decision Step of Referee Report?

The Decision step of Referee Report shows three figures: the editor's **Decision**, the number of **Required Revisions**, and **Referees Who Recommended It**, how many of the referees who reported recommended the same decision. Below, the editor's one-sentence reason and **Open the Letter**, then a table of each referee's role, recommendation, number of major concerns and minor points, and the one-sentence reason from its `report.toml`. A decision that few referees recommended is not wrong for that, since the editor weighs arguments, but its reason is in **The Decision** and **Where the Referees Disagree** of the letter, which are worth reading first.

## What Did Former Editors Find Referees Get Wrong?

[Berk, Harvey and Hirshleifer (2017)](#ref-BHH2017a) wrote to former editors of the American Economic Review, the Journal of Political Economy, the Quarterly Journal of Economics, Econometrica, the Review of Economic Studies and the Journal of Financial Economics, and found a rough consensus that referees of top economics journals make similar mistakes, which can be corrected. They argue that much of the time spent on revisions, with their many robustness checks and extensions, is a waste of research effort, and give a roadmap for avoiding the mistakes. The skills of Referee Report ask each referee to say, for every major concern, why it matters for the paper's claims and what would resolve it, and to leave out what does not change its assessment.
